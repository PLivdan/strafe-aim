A visual guide Moving while you aim, in first-person shooters

He is not moving that fast He is moving ten units a second, the same as you, the same as everybody. Whether that is an easy target or an impossible one is decided by which two keys you are holding.

Both fights below use the same enemy, the same dodge, the same range, the same weapon, and the same hand on the mouse. The only difference is what your feet are doing.

Blue is you and Red is the enemy, here and everywhere else on this page.

One of these is a fight about mouse control. The other is barely a fight at all.

This is a companion to Strafe Aiming 101, published free by AIMER7 in August 2019. The framework is his, the vocabulary is his, and every classification on this page is his. What is added here is arithmetic: the guide argues in prose and asks you to picture it, and pictures are exactly the thing that is hard to hold in your head about two people moving at once.

So everything here runs. The figures are not illustrations of numbers computed elsewhere; they are two players, two crosshairs and a clock, and the numbers underneath are read off the same simulation you are watching. Where the guide makes a checkable claim, this page checks it rather than repeating it.

It also borrows, gratefully, from Fundamentals of Strafe by Sam, which is the best short answer to the question the technical guide deliberately leaves alone: what all of this is for.

Speed is ten units per second throughout, which is the guide's round number and not any particular game's. Every figure runs by itself and stops when it scrolls off the screen. Most of them can be swapped to the other player's monitor, and most of the value is in doing that.

What is assumed

The eight movement directions are equivalent: same speed, same acceleration. You are holding a tracking weapon with infinite ammunition, no spread and no recoil. Nobody is jumping.

None of that is true of any real game. All of it can be relaxed once the shape of the thing is clear, and the guide is explicit that this is a framework for thinking rather than a set of instructions.

Part one

What a change of direction costs

Start with the thing that goes wrong. You are tracking someone cleanly. He changes direction. For a moment your crosshair keeps going the way it was going, and by the time you have hauled it back he is somewhere else. Everybody has this fight. It is worth being precise about where the time actually goes, because the answer is in two parts and only one of them is under your control.

A dumb but sufficient definition of aim: putting a crosshair somewhere, at a time of your choosing. That splits into two different skills that people constantly confuse.

The first is your hand. Mouse control is the smoothness of your motion, your straight lines and your curves at every speed, your flicks of different lengths and accelerations, your ability to land out of a flick and keep tracking, your micro-corrections. It is tested and built standing still, which is why aim trainers are good at it. If you have good mouse control in a game, you have good mouse control in Paint.

The second is your eyes, and it is not one skill either. The guide splits reading three ways:

  • Precision reading — how exactly you can tell where the crosshair sits relative to the body. The spatial resolution of your attention.
  • Fast-strafes reading — how often you refresh that judgement. A continuous reaction time rather than a single one. When it is poor the scene stays blurry long after a click-test says your reactions are fine, and a blurry scene gives you nothing to correct towards, so you either miss or hit by luck. Perfect mouse control does not help.
  • Movement reading — knowing how much mouse a given change of direction is going to demand, given how both of you are moving. This is the knowledge-based one, it is the one this entire guide trains, and it is the one an aim trainer with a predictable bot lets you skip entirely.

Aim at a target while standing still and movement reading is nearly free: there are two speeds to choose between, ten units per second to the left and ten to the right. Start moving and the combination of his motion and yours produces a variety of apparent speeds and accelerations, and suddenly the reading is the hard part.

Definition

Reactivity, and its two halves

Suppose your mouse control were perfect and your reading were at its genetic ceiling. You would still not have perfect aim, because you cannot react instantly. The ideal correction after a change of direction is an instant flick with an instant stop — teleport the crosshair back and lock. You cannot do that. The time from his change of direction to your crosshair being back on him is reactivity, and it has two parts:

  • The reactive part — how long before you notice you are missing, or that he has turned. It belongs to your reading, and it is measurable.
  • The correcting part — how long the mouse takes to get back once you have noticed. Also measurable: on a slow-motion video it is the stretch where the crosshair is accelerating.

It is hard to be smooth and reactive at once, because smoothness is the absence of large accelerations. Which one to favour depends on the game and on you. Broadly, smoothness builds mouse control faster, but if your reaction times are slow you will do better with a reactive style than a smooth one.

Here is the lever this entire guide turns on. The reactive part is nearly fixed. You cannot press a key that makes you notice sooner. But the size of the gap you have to close — and therefore the length of the correcting part — depends on where you were moving while you were not noticing. That is a choice, and you make it eight times a second.

Measuring your own reactivity

AIMER7's test, for anyone with KovaaK's and an hour:

  1. Launch tracking RT test.
  2. Record it at 120 fps.
  3. Track the bot as smoothly as you can without predicting the change of direction. When it turns, flick back as fast as you can while keeping the tracking reasonably smooth. Thirty seconds to a minute.
  4. Open the recording in VLC and step frame by frame with E.
  5. Start counting at the first frame where the crosshair is off the bot after a change of direction. Stop at the middle of the correcting part — the moment the crosshair is not moving relative to the background, just before you accelerate it back.
  6. Repeat for every change of direction and average.
  7. Your reactivity is the average frame count times 8.33 ms.

The figure above is a model, not a measurement of you. Put your own number into it and the rest of the page is calibrated to your hands.

Part two

The rule is one sentence

Everything from here is one idea, applied over and over.

Definition

To strafe aim is to change direction whenever, and only whenever, the target you are aiming at changes direction.

AIMER7, Strafe Aiming 101, §2.2

Read what it constrains. Not your mouse — your feet. Strafe aiming says nothing about how you move the crosshair. It says when you are allowed to change direction, and the answer is: exactly when he does, and never on your own initiative.

That makes it a fundamentally reactive style. You are giving up every opportunity to change direction in order to make him miss, and spending all of it on maximising your own damage instead. It is not a virtue and it is not a default; it is a trade, and the rest of the page is about when the trade is worth making.

Try it. Press ← and → to change direction. Answer every change of his, and invent none of your own. It is harder than it sounds, and the two ways of failing are counted separately.

The two failures

Missing his change is the obvious one. He turned, you did not, and the form you thought you were playing is not the form you played.

Changing on your own is the one nobody counts. It is not a mistake in itself — moving with your own intent is most of what good players do — but it is not strafe aiming, and if you cannot tell the difference you cannot practise either.

Holding your direction straight through his change has a name. It is called a long dodge, it is advanced and situational, and we will come back to it.

The one target it cannot be held against

Since you cannot react instantly, it is impossible to strafe aim a target that changes direction faster than you can notice. That is what short-dodging is, or ad-spamming, or whatever your game calls it. There is no form that beats it, because there is no form you can execute in time.

What beats it is refusing to play. With decent reading you can tell someone is short-dodging within two or three hundred milliseconds, and from then on you can put your crosshair in the middle of his oscillation and collect him twice a cycle without locking onto him at all. He is doing the aiming for you.

Which is why short-dodging for any length of time is a bad movement form, and also why a single short dodge dropped into a longer pattern is an excellent one: nobody has perfect reading, and a fake that arrives before you have re-read the picture will beat you. It is not strafe aiming when you do it. That is fine. Not everything should be.

Drag the slider. Somewhere around a quarter of a second the better strategy flips, and where exactly it flips is a fact about your reaction time rather than about principle.

Part three

Eight keys, and where you are looking

There are eight movement directions. When he changes direction you are forced to change too, and since you are already moving in one of them you are choosing from the other seven. Eight starting directions, seven answers: fifty-six strafe aim forms, for each change of direction of his.

Before that number can mean anything, two small things have to be nailed down, and they are the two things people get wrong for years.

First: a key is named in the frame of whoever presses it. He is facing you. His left hand points where your right hand points. So "he goes left" and "you go left" are opposite directions in the world, and pressing the same key as him is not mirroring him — it is the opposite of mirroring him. By convention throughout, the enemy going to his left appears to move to the right across your monitor.

Second: the keys are named relative to where you are looking. That direction is the view-angle: where the crosshair points. Hold forward and you move exactly where you are looking. Hold backward and you move at a half turn from it. Hold a diagonal and you move at a quarter or three-quarter turn. Since you are looking at him, holding a key fixes the angle between your movement and the line between the two of you, and that angle is the only thing that matters.

Split any movement into two parts and everything simplifies. The part running across the line of sight is what your mouse has to follow. The part running along it changes the range, which changes how much mouse a given speed across costs. Ten units per second across is a wide sweep up close and a lazy drift at distance.

Press every key. Two of the eight leave him nearly motionless on your monitor. Two make him cross it at twice his own speed. The rest are in between, and the number at the bottom right is the whole of the next section.

The two components

Along it→how much mouse that costs

The guide talks in relative speed rather than screen speed because screen speed depends on the range and relative speed does not. Both are on every figure here.

Part four

The eight fundamental forms

Fifty-six is a lot, so classify them by the angle between your two directions. The most fundamental are the ones where that angle is a half turn: your two directions are exact opposites, and you are oscillating along a line. There are eight of them, in four families, and they are the vocabulary everything else is built from.

Mirroring, and the thing people get wrong about it

Mirroring is going right whenever he goes left, and left whenever he goes right. Because he is facing you, that means you are both travelling the same way through the world, and the relative speed across the sight line is zero. He does not move on your monitor at all.

A great many players believe mirroring is strafe aiming. It is one of eight, and it is the one where nobody has to use any mouse control whatsoever.

Anti-mirroring is pressing the same keys he does, which sends you the opposite way through the world and puts the relative speed at twenty — the highest available. You are constantly moving the mouse, which means you are constantly using mouse control, and so is he.

The half-sideways forms use the diagonals, so only part of your speed runs across the sight line and the rest opens or closes the range. There are two of each. The anti-mirroring versions oppose him at 17.07 units per second. The mirroring versions follow him at 2.93, which is close enough to nothing that the target is very nearly motionless — and that is exactly why they are the hardest of all to read.

Back and forth puts nothing at all across the sight line. It is almost the same as standing still, and it is almost as easy to hit.

Hold each one and watch two things. The trace under the monitor is what your mouse has to do. Then swap the camera and look at what the same form is doing to his.

What each one is actually like

Mirroring. Movement reading is trivial, because outside the changes of direction the relative speed is zero and there is nothing to read. During a change it briefly reaches twenty. The ideal correction is an instant flick with an instant stop, which is hard when the target is small on your screen, and if you fail it you now have to beat static friction to micro-correct, which is miserable. Worse: the situation is symmetric. He does not need to move his mouse either. If your mouse control is better than his, mirroring throws that advantage away and turns the fight into a straight trade — which is fine if you have more health, and terrible if you do not. Against a target moving vertically it is unpleasant to read, because you have to know exactly when to stop the mouse horizontally and when to nudge it.

Anti-mirroring. Movement reading is relatively easy because the speed is a constant twenty. Mouse control is not: being smooth and precise at that speed is difficult, especially close in. But you are using your mouse control at all times and forcing him to use his, which leverages any edge you have. It beats static friction continuously, so micro-corrections are easier. It plays better against vertical movement than mirroring does. And because it is at maximum speed and pointing the wrong way, it rewards reactivity.

Half-sideways anti-mirroring. Reading is harder, because the apparent speed changes as the range opens and closes. Mouse control is harder than plain anti-mirroring, since you now need real precision in the horizontal acceleration. The relative speed, 17.07, is a little slower. It shares anti-mirroring's advantages, and it adds a specific cost: on a diagonal your speed across the map is lower than his, so you are easier for him to read than he is for you. Most players are not smooth enough to track diagonals properly. It makes vertical angles easier and it makes you harder to hit from above, because you are moving in two dimensions rather than one.

Half-sideways mirroring. The hardest of the eight on both reading and mouse control, because it demands precision, smoothness and control of small accelerations all at once. The relative speed is 2.93 — the target is almost motionless. That is why it is hard, and it is why so many players watching a good strafe aimer conclude that his mouse is not moving. It is nearly symmetric, so it is also very hard for him if he lacks precision, though reading you is still slightly easier for him than reading him is for you. It is by far the best form for precision at long range, because it cancels most of his apparent motion, and it beats static friction at all times because you are always moving the mouse a little. Being inward-directed, it maximises your damage output better than anything else, by shrinking his relative velocity the most.

Back and forth. A mixed form. It trivialises reading — it is nearly standing still — and it makes you easy to hit, but it allows extremely precise micro-corrections. It has real uses: when you are safe behind a shield and aiming at a distant target, or in a game with knockback, where pushing forward as someone falls and pulling back as the knockback lifts them is the best way to maximise damage. It also dodges people to your side for free. Outside those cases it is not worth training for its own sake.

Read this table sideways

Nothing in this list says a form is good. Each one says which skill the fight will be decided by, and who that suits.

A slow form is a fight about precision. A fast form is a fight about smoothness and speed. Mirroring is a fight about nothing at all, which means it is a fight about health.

Part five

Inward and outward

Relative speed tells you how hard the tracking is between changes of direction. It does not tell you what the changes themselves cost, and that is where the damage actually goes.

Here is the test. He changes direction. Freeze your mouse — you have not noticed yet, so it is going to keep doing whatever it was doing anyway. Now: does the gap between your crosshair and his body get smaller over the next fifth of a second, or bigger?

If your new direction carries you across the sight line the same way he is now going, you are following him through the world, the sight line swings towards where your crosshair already is, and the correction you will owe when you finally notice is smaller. The guide calls that change of direction inward-directed. If you go the other way, everything above runs backwards: the gap opens faster, the flick you owe is longer, and a longer flick is also a less accurate one. That is outward-directed.

A whole form is inward-directed when both of its changes are, outward-directed when both are not, and mixed otherwise.

Back and forth is the interesting case, because neither of its directions moves you across the sight line at all and the test above is silent. What separates its two halves is the range. Push in and you shorten it, so the same speed across becomes a faster sweep across your screen — outward. Pull back and the opposite — inward. Small, but real, and the figure measures it.

This figure is the experiment, not a diagram of it. Every bar is one key, held for one reaction time, with nobody touching the mouse. Shorter is better. The dashed line is what standing still would have cost you.

One warning about symmetry

Strictly, whether a change is inward or outward depends on which way he turned as well as which way you did. Assuming perfect reading, every change of his is equivalent on your monitor to a left-right change, which is why the whole thing can be discussed with two of his directions rather than eight.

Be careful with that equivalence in practice: a form that is inward-directed against a left-right dodge can be mixed or outward against a diagonal one.

Transferable rule

You cannot notice faster. You can decide, in advance, how large the gap will be when you do notice — and it is decided by the direction you take, not by the mouse you have.

This is why the same player, with the same hands, hits far more with one pair of keys than another against an identical enemy.

Part six

A form does not make you hit more

It is tempting to read the last two sections as a ranking, with half-sideways mirroring at the top. That is the wrong lesson, and the simulation says so plainly.

Run the same twenty seconds with both players reacting equally well and with the same hands, and mirroring puts both of you at a hundred per cent. Nobody has to move their mouse, so nobody misses, and nobody wins. Whether that is a triumph or a disaster depends entirely on your health bar.

Every form is doing this. It is not deciding how much damage you deal; it is deciding which skill the fight is about, and therefore whose fight it is. A fast form is a fight about smoothness at speed. A slow one is a fight about precision and micro-correction. Mirroring is a fight about neither, which makes it a fight about resources.

So the useful question is never "which form is best". It is: given what I am better at, and what he is better at, which skill do I want this fight to be about?

Give yourself the better hand and watch which bars move. Mirroring barely twitches, which is exactly the guide's warning: if your mouse control is better than his, mirroring throws it away.

When trading is correct

More health than him. Higher damage per second. A teammate arriving. Anything that means the clock is on your side. Mirroring is then not lazy, it is the answer.

When it is a disaster

Even health and a better hand than his. You have just declined to use the only advantage you had, and agreed to a coin flip.

“The most simple form is the mirror… Ask yourself when this would be useful? Two answers are when you have more HP, or a higher dps weapon. You don’t mind trading hits in these cases, since your enemy will die before you do.”

Part seven

What all eight have in common

Every one of the eight fundamental forms has the same flaw, and it is a serious one.

If he goes left as much as he goes right — and most players do, including professionals — then so do you. Your two directions are exact opposites and they cancel. Four seconds of any 180-form leaves you on the spot you started from. They are globally static movement styles.

Which means that while you are maximising damage against this one person, you cannot reposition. You cannot follow your team, take a position, break a sight line, or get out of the way of the second enemy who is not the one you are aiming at. In a team game that is close to disqualifying.

There is a second cost, quieter and specific to the diagonals. In most games the diagonals are no faster than the cardinals, so a half-sideways form moves you across the map more slowly than his plain left-right dodge moves him. He reads you more easily than you read him. And sooner or later your body passes straight through where his crosshair already is, and he gets a free hit for having done nothing at all.

You want to avoid giving free shots even when your stated goal is to maximise damage. It is very unlikely you can actually afford to ignore everything else.

The arithmetic

Two opposite directions, held for equal times, sum to zero. That is not a tendency, it is a subtraction, and it is why the fix has to come from somewhere outside the eight.

The fix is the quarter turns, and they are the next section.

Part eight

Connections

Two forms are connected when they share a prescription: when there is one of his directions for which both forms want the same key from you. That shared key is the connection, and it is a door. At the moment you pass through it, both forms want exactly what you are already holding, so you can leave one and enter the other without ever breaking the rule.

Every form is connected to itself, which is what lets you repeat one indefinitely. And here is the fact that explains the last section: no two different 180-forms are connected. Not one pair. Eight islands. That is why nothing built from them alone can go anywhere.

Bring in the quarter turns and the whole set becomes a graph you can walk. The useful ones are the 90-forms that connect to the fundamental ones:

  • Forward triangle. Two of them. The mirroring version — forward-right when he goes left, forward-left when he goes right — is inward-directed. The anti-mirroring version swaps them and is outward-directed. Neither can be held forever, because you arrive.
  • Backward triangle. The same pair, backwards. Also inward and outward respectively. Neither can be held forever, because you leave.
  • Back-and-forth triangle. Four of them, all mixed, because you only ever strafe to one side. Held properly, your track on the ground is a circle and you always aim at its centre. They are hard on precision, and because you are genuinely travelling you need to be comfortable with large angles.

An intermediate form is a connection, or a chain of connections, of fundamental forms and these blocks. Chain a backward mirroring triangle to a half-sideways mirror to a forward mirroring triangle and you have a back-and-forth mirroring triangle, left-directed or right-directed depending on how you started it, and inward-directed throughout because every part of it is. Use the anti-mirroring versions and you get the anti-mirroring one, outward throughout. There is a rhombus family too, clockwise or anticlockwise, with different parts of the shape inward or outward depending on how you assemble it.

Build one. Only forms that actually connect to where you are are offered, and the key you pass through is written on the arrow. Watch the track close into a rhombus, or open into a circle around him.

Two exercises from the guide

Can you find a way to generate a back-and-forth top or bottom triangle through connections?

Can you produce one rhombus of each orientation, and say which part of each is inward-directed?

Both are buildable in the figure. The chain builder will not let you make an illegal move, so if you can reach it, it is a genuine chain of connections.

Why bother listing them

Movement reading is knowledge-based. Understanding why two shapes that look alike — two triangles, two rhombi — demand completely different corrections is the training. The more precisely you can think about how to move the mouse in an abstract case, the better you perform in a real one.

Part nine

Advanced forms

A quick summary of where we are. The fundamental forms are the eight half-turns. They are completely static, which forbids repositioning. Connections let the quarter turns generate the intermediate forms, and those are the first ones that travel: some push forward, some retreat, some circle him. But they are mostly half-sideways, and half-sideways never maximises either the relative speed from his point of view or your speed across the map, which leaves you slightly easier to hit than he is. The only two non-diagonal forms in the intermediate set are plain mirroring and plain anti-mirroring.

Good movement has a purpose. It maximises damage dealt, or minimises damage taken, or repositions you to somewhere that is worth being. No movement does all three. The advanced forms are the ones chosen for a specific fight, and several of them are quite good at not being hit while still dealing a serious amount of damage.

What makes them advanced is not the shape. It is that they are argued from what he will do next. Each of the three below provokes a particular response and is already holding the answer to it. That needs timing, and a decent intuition for how ordinary players react when their mouse control is being stretched. Good dodgers are not so predictable, because they are reacting to your reactions, and so on down.

Notice something about the first one especially: his change of direction is predictable, and you are still strafe aiming. That is the signature of an advanced form.

Nothing. The building blocks are exactly the fifty-six forms we counted at the start. An advanced strafe aim form is a connection, or a chain of connections, of those fifty-six. That is the whole framework, and it is quite a lot.

Part ten

Movement with a purpose

Everything so far has treated the other player as a dodge script. He is not one, and the last part of this is about what changes when you remember that.

An obvious requirement for strafing well is knowing what your strafe is meant to achieve. The simple goals are: deal the most damage, take the least, get somewhere on the map. The advanced ones are things like holding a good geometric position, dodging a second enemy to your side while keeping a decent form against the one in front, or pushing at a particular moment to draw a particular response. You rarely have exactly one goal. More often you have a hierarchy of them with shifting weights, and the weights change several times a second.

So do not treat strafe aiming as something you commit to for a fight. It is the default you fall back on, and it should be broken frequently and on purpose. You will merge in and out of it constantly. The way to earn that fluency is to practise the forms until they are automatic, so that when you are doing something else — taking an angle, dodging, arriving somewhere — the strafe aiming is still running underneath without your attention.

The artful dodger

Sam's picture of the player worth becoming: “a lazy old man… He has an intention with every keypress. The artful dodger puts himself in situations where he has to do little, while his enemy must do a lot.”

Which is a fair description of an inward-directed form held from a position he cannot leave.

Global bias

If there is a single quick fix for a bad dodge, it is this: over a few seconds, go one way more than the other. Do not finish the fight standing where you started it.

A dodge with no bias has a middle, and a middle is a place someone can leave a crosshair. He does not have to track you at all; he under-aims at the centre of your oscillation and collects you twice a cycle for free. Introduce a bias and that stops working, because the centre keeps walking away from wherever he left the crosshair — and he now has to cover a larger angle as well. So do you, which is the honest cost.

The same argument condemns the jump patterns that use strictly zero global bias. They are countered by correct strafing and a crosshair that waits.

The other reason to have a bias is that it is the only way to relocate mid-fight: to hide an angle, to expose one, to move relative to a teammate, to reach the position you already know is better. This is where the 90-forms and the chains from the last section pay for themselves.

Drag his bias to zero and watch the thick orange appear. That is a crosshair sitting still, collecting free damage.

“This is why a good dodge is a dodge that tries to move globally in one direction (modulated by local change of directions) for a relatively long time-scale. This is also why adad spam is the poorest dodge you can do.”

Geometry, and control

None of this happens in an empty plane. Where the two of you are standing decides how much room he has to move into, how much of him you can see, and who can end the fight. That is a whole separate subject and it has its own guide; there is a visual companion to that one too. A few things worth noticing while you strafe:

  • If he is in a corridor you never need to aim closer to the wall than one body width. His options are the map's, not his.
  • Your position decides how much screen his movement can occupy. Distance to an opening decides how much space he has on your monitor.
  • Distance to a corner decides who sees whom first, and who gets to cancel the fight.
  • Dodging into a wall has a psychological effect: he expects you to come back out. Use it.
  • Players feel unreasonably safe next to cover, because they can hide at any moment. They dodge there with no bias at all, and spamming beside the cover collects a great deal for free.
  • When someone wants to hide, he usually goes to the cover in a straight line.

All of those have a mirror image where you are the one being read.

Control is the same idea one level up. Probe, find what he is bad at, push there, and withdraw where he is solid. Push at a moment and an angle he cannot hit, and if he is not hitting, keep going. When you move in a way that is hard to aim at, he will use his own movement to make aiming easier — and at that moment you decided what he did. When he tries to force something and you defend well, he has to cancel, and you decided that too.

The reverse matters just as much: learn to aim independently of your movement. If you always mirror him the instant aiming gets hard, you are the one being led.

Calm, and why it is a technique

Most players have a badly warped sense of what their opponent can actually do. In most situations his options are quite limited and they span a small range of outcomes.

Flicking manically while ad-spamming and jumping every other second makes the picture on your own screen erratic and fast, which makes it harder to see, which produces more panic. A player who strafe aims, moves the mouse efficiently and positions well is looking at a slower, smaller, more predictable picture — of the same fight.

Calm is downstream of that, not a personality trait. It also requires confidence, because a long strafe or a still mouse in the middle of his dodge does not feel like you are doing anything, even when it is the best play available.

Part eleven

Practice

Coupling aim to movement is a long, difficult process and nobody should expect it to come quickly. The routines below assume you are already comfortably sub-intermediate on ordinary aim work; if you are not, that comes first.

The goal of practice is to take an idea you are more or less convinced of and develop a feel for it. You have to be actively thinking while you do it — deliberately strafe aiming, deliberately biasing your dodge, deliberately taking an angle, one thing at a time. One-versus-ones lend themselves to this because there is a pause between rounds in which to decide what the next one is for.

Two pieces of advice from Sam that are worth more than any scenario list. Surround yourself with better players, and simply play them; you do not need them to be good teachers. And find a context where you do not mind looking bad — an alias, an unranked queue, anything — because the practice that works is the practice where you try things that do not work yet.

Watching top players helps, but do not analyse details until you are already strong. Take in the feel of their fights and try to reproduce that instead.

Before the routines

Bind the aimbot to your fire key and play in free play. The aimbot has perfect mouse control, so it shows you exactly what the mouse is supposed to do for a form you are trying to learn. This is the fastest way to build movement reading that exists.

What the scoring systems reward

This matters more than it sounds, because a scoring system is a training partner that never gets tired and never changes its mind. If it rewards the wrong movement you will learn the wrong movement, very efficiently.

None of the movement scoring systems currently available is good for this. Which means score is not especially relevant in most of the maps below, and the routines only work if you are honest and are not trying to cheese them.

The third column is the one that does not exist yet. It has been offered to at least one aim trainer developer, free, for several years.

The one that would work

Conditional movement scoring. Points only for following a prescription — this key while the bot goes left, that key while it goes right. Award nothing in the first 200 ms after the bot turns, so prediction stops paying.

That is a scoring system for strafe aim forms, and it is the whole idea.

Part twelve

The lab

Every form, both hands, either camera, nothing decided for you. Two numbers are worth watching: the trade, and the worst gap per change of direction. A form that improves your gap while leaving the trade level is a form that is not doing anything for you.

The arithmetic

Relative speed across the sight line

Put the line of sight along x. You look along it, he looks back along it, and his left hand points where your right hand points. A key held by either player becomes a world vector by rotating it into that player's frame. Split both velocities into a part across the sight line and a part along it. Only the first is what the mouse must follow.

vacross=|s·sinθhis−s·sinθyours|

with s the ground speed and θ each key's angle from its own player's forward. Because he is facing you, his contribution and yours enter with opposite signs, which is why pressing the same key as him doubles the speed and pressing the opposite one cancels it.

For the eight half-turn forms this gives the guide's five numbers exactly. Mirroring cancels: 10−10=0. Anti-mirroring adds: 10+10=20. A diagonal only puts 10cos45°=7.07 across the sight line, so the half-sideways forms land at 10±7.07. Back and forth puts nothing across at all, so it sits at his bare ten.

From units per second to your mousepad

Relative speed is range-free, which is why the guide uses it. What your hand experiences is the angular rate, and that is the relative speed divided by the range.

ω=vacrossd,centimetres per second=ω·180/π360·cm per 360

Halve the range and everything doubles. This is the whole reason a form that is comfortable across a room is unplayable in someone's face, and why half-sideways mirroring — which shrinks vacross the most — is the guide's pick for long range in particular.

Counting the fifty-six

You hold one of eight keys while he goes left and one of the remaining seven while he goes right, so there are 8×7=56 ordered pairs. Classified by the angle between your two directions, each starting direction has one opposite, two neighbours at each of 45°, 90° and 135°, giving 8+16+16+16. The site's tests check that census, and check that no two of the eight half-turn forms share a prescription.

Inward and outward, as a measurement

The classification is usually stated as a rule with cases. It does not have to be. Freeze the crosshair at the instant he reverses, hold a candidate key for one reaction time, and record the angle between the crosshair and his body. Do the same holding nothing at all. Anything that finishes closer than standing still is inward-directed; anything further is outward-directed.

That single measurement reproduces every classification in the guide, including the awkward one: back and forth comes out mixed, because forward shortens the range and backward lengthens it, and the sweep across your screen goes as one over the range.

The aim model

Both players run the same controller and it has four moving parts. Between changes of direction it matches the apparent angular rate it saw one reaction time ago, with a light trim, which is why a form with a near-zero rate costs almost nothing to hold. A change of direction is invisible for one reaction time, so the gap opens unopposed: that is the reactive part. Then a single ballistic flick, aimed once at where he is estimated to be by the time it lands, whose duration is the gap divided by the correction speed: that is the correcting part. The flick misses by a fixed fraction of its own size, so a larger correction is also a worse one. Tracking wobble is a settling random walk whose size grows with how fast the mouse is being asked to move, which is the only place "mouse control" enters the model at all.

Nothing in the controller knows what a form is. The eight forms are inputs, and the ranking that comes out — mirroring, then half-sideways mirroring, then back and forth, then the two anti-mirrors — is the guide's ranking, produced rather than encoded.

What the model leaves out

  • No walls, no height, no cover. Positioning is a different guide.
  • No jumping, no air control, no game-specific acceleration curves. The eight directions are equal, as the guide assumes.
  • No projectile travel time, no spread, no recoil, no reload. Time on target stands in for damage.
  • Movement keys are resolved against the true bearing rather than the lagging crosshair. Using the crosshair is more literal, and in an empty plane it produces a slow outward spiral that has nothing to do with strafe aiming. The lab has a note where this matters.
  • The enemy is a dodge script with a period and a bias. He does not read you, and a real one does.
Reproducing the guide

The checkable claims are pinned by a test suite, so they cannot quietly stop being true:

  • the five relative speeds, exactly
  • the census: 8 + 16 + 16 + 16 = 56
  • no two half-turn forms are connected
  • every inward, outward and mixed label in §2.3, §3.1 and §3.2, derived from components and confirmed by the frozen-mouse probe
  • all eight half-turn forms have zero net displacement; the 90-forms do not
  • parking on the middle beats tracking against a short dodge, and loses against a long one

Where the arithmetic and the prose differ

Measuring something that was argued in prose occasionally produces a different answer. Sometimes the prose was loose. More often the two are defining a term slightly differently. Each one is set out so you can judge it yourself.

Take this away

You cannot notice faster than you notice. What you can decide, before he moves, is how big the gap will be when you do. That decision is two keys wide: one for when he goes left, one for when he goes right. Pick them for what they do to the gap, for which skill they make the fight about, and for where they leave you standing when the fight is over.
