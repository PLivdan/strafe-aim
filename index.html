A visual guide Moving while you aim, in first-person shooters

Blue is you, Red is the enemy, everywhere on this page. Orange means your crosshair is on them.

Your mouse is not the only thing controlling how hard a target is to track.

Your movement changes their movement on your screen.

This guide is about learning to control that relationship. Everything in it runs live — every figure is a small simulated fight, and every number underneath is measured from the fight you are watching. Where the ideas come from, and how the simulation works, is written up at the back of the page.

Part one

Why a direction change makes you miss

You are tracking someone smoothly. They change direction.

For a fraction of a second, your mouse keeps going the old way — you cannot react instantly, and during that delay nothing you do helps, because you have not yet noticed anything is wrong. By the time you react, your crosshair is behind them.

Now you have to correct. The farther they got from your crosshair during that delay, the bigger the correction you owe. Call that distance the gap. It shows up in yellow in every figure on this page.

The figure to the right runs this over and over, and times it. The bar underneath splits each miss into its two parts: the time before you noticed, and the time your mouse spent travelling back.

Here is the fact the whole guide rests on. You cannot make the first part shorter. No key on your keyboard makes you notice sooner. But look at what happens to the gap when you switch what you are doing with your feet: the same reaction delay produces a large correction in one case and almost none in another.

Try it. Switch between standing still and the other options, and watch the yellow number. Your reaction time never changes. The correction you owe does.

Rule

You cannot react sooner. But your movement decides how much correction you owe when you do react.

Where the time goes

Noticing takes most people around 150–250 ms. It is close to fixed — sleep affects it, keys do not.

Correcting takes as long as the gap is big. That is the part this guide is about, and it is decided before you ever notice you are missing.

Part two

Move with them, or against them

So how does your movement change the gap? Start with the simplest possible case.

The enemy dodges left and right. You have two options: when they move, travel the same way through the world, or travel the opposite way.

Try both. Ignore accuracy for now. Watch only how fast they travel across your screen.

Move with them and their motion across your screen shrinks — you are both drifting the same way, so from where you stand they barely move. Move against them and their motion across your screen grows: your speed and theirs add together.

Your feet just changed how much work your mouse had to do. Nothing about the enemy changed at all.

These two choices have names. Moving with them is called mirroring — confusingly, you press the opposite keys to theirs, because you are facing each other, and that is what makes you travel the same way. Moving against them is called anti-mirroring — you press the same keys they do. A lot of players get this backwards for years. The world direction is what matters, not the key.

Why the keys feel backwards

You are facing each other, so their left hand points where your right hand points. When they press their left-strafe key and you press yours, you move in opposite world directions. To travel with someone who is facing you, press the key on the other side.

Part three

The rule behind strafe aiming

In the last figure the simulation was changing direction for you, at exactly the right moments. That timing is the actual skill, and it has a name.

Strafe aiming takes the choice you just made one step further: instead of changing direction whenever you feel like it, you respond to the enemy's changes — every one of them, and only them.

The definition

To strafe aim is to change direction whenever, and only whenever, the target you are aiming at changes direction.

AIMER7, Strafe Aiming 101, §2.2

Notice what the rule constrains: your feet, not your mouse. It says nothing about how to move the crosshair. And it has two halves, which fail in two different ways. Missing one of their changes is the obvious failure. Changing direction on your own initiative is the other — it is not a mistake in itself, and later we will see good reasons to do it, but it is not strafe aiming, and if you cannot tell the difference you cannot practise either one.

Try it. Press ← and → below. Answer every change of theirs, and invent none of your own. It is harder than it sounds, and the score counts the two failures separately.

What you give up

Strafe aiming is a reactive style. You are spending every direction change on your own aim, and none of them on making yourself harder to hit. That is a trade, not a virtue — the rest of the guide is about when it is worth making.

When the rule cannot be followed

There is one target the rule simply cannot be applied to: someone reversing faster than you can notice. If they change direction every 150 milliseconds and you need 200 to see it, there is no answer you can give in time. No form fixes this.

What works instead is refusing to chase. Once you recognise the pattern — which takes most people two or three hundred milliseconds — place your crosshair near the centre of their dodge and let them cross it twice on each full cycle. They are doing the aiming for you.

This is why spamming left-right for any length of time is a weak way to move: it hands out free hits to anyone patient enough to stop tracking. And it is why a single fast reversal, dropped into a longer pattern, is excellent — nobody re-reads the picture that fast, and a fake that arrives before they have looked again will land.

Drag the slider. Somewhere around a quarter of a second the better strategy flips. Where it flips for you depends on your reaction time, not on principle.

Why fast reversals are unreadable

Seeing is not continuous. You re-read where the crosshair sits against the target a handful of times a second, and anything that happens between two readings arrives late and smeared. The small figure below the comparison makes this visible.

Part four

Your movement changes their speed on your screen

Why does moving with someone make them easier to track? You already know the answer from ordinary life.

Imagine someone running past you at 10 mph. Now start running beside them at 10 mph. Relative to you, they barely move — you could hold a conversation. Turn around and run the other way, and they fly past you.

Nothing about their speed changed. Your movement changed their speed relative to you. Aiming works exactly the same way, because your screen shows the world relative to you.

One refinement makes the whole thing precise. What matters to your mouse is not their full relative speed, but the part of it that runs across your line of sight — that is the part that slides the target sideways on your screen. Movement along the line of sight changes the range instead, which changes how big a sweep the same sideways speed produces: the same speed is a wide, fast sweep up close and a lazy drift at distance.

Try every key. The figure shows both players from above with their movement split into those two parts, and the number at the bottom is the one your mouse cares about. Two keys nearly cancel their motion. Two keys double it. The rest land in between.

Part five

There are more than two ways to do this

So far you have moved left and right. But you have more keys than that. What happens if you answer their dodge by moving forward, backward, or on a diagonal?

A pair of answers — one direction for when they go left, one for when they go right — is called a form. Mirroring and anti-mirroring are the two you know. The other important ones use the diagonals, and they are worth meeting the same way: by what they feel like, when they help, and what they give up.

If both players move at 10 units per second, these come out as 0, 2.93, 10, 17.07 and 20 across your screen. The numbers confirm the feel; you do not need them to play.

Mirroringnearly frozen

What it feels like. The enemy nearly freezes on your screen. Neither of you has to move the mouse except at their direction changes.

Use it when you are happy to trade hits — more health, more damage per second, a teammate arriving.

Cost. It freezes you on their screen too. If your mouse control is better than theirs, mirroring throws that advantage away.

Anti-mirroringfastest

What it feels like. The enemy races across your screen at twice their own speed, and your mouse is always moving.

Use it when you trust your high-speed tracking more than theirs — it forces both players to use it constantly.

Cost. Both players have to aim hard. Being smooth and precise at this speed is genuinely difficult, especially up close.

Half-sideways mirroringslow

What it feels like. Mirroring on a diagonal. The target is almost motionless — which sounds easy and is actually the hardest form to aim with, because tiny speeds demand real precision, and the range keeps changing.

Use it when you need precision at long range — it cancels most of their apparent motion — or when your fine control is your strength.

Cost. Heavy on precision for both players. On a diagonal you also cover ground more slowly than their straight dodge, so you are a little easier to read than they are.

Half-sideways anti-mirroringfast

What it feels like. Anti-mirroring on a diagonal: fast, and the speed keeps changing as the range opens and closes.

Use it when you want anti-mirroring's constant pressure but need to handle height or close distance at the same time — moving in two dimensions makes you harder to hit from above.

Cost. Harder to read than plain anti-mirroring, and most players are not smooth enough to track diagonals well. That cuts both ways.

Back and forththeir own speed

What it feels like. You move only towards and away from them. Sideways, it is almost the same as standing still.

Use it when you are safe — behind a shield, out of range — and want the steadiest possible platform for precise corrections. Also sidesteps people attacking from your flank.

Cost. You are nearly a stationary target. Not worth training for its own sake.

And the rest

Count the possibilities: eight directions you might be moving in when they turn, and seven you could switch to. That makes 56 possible response pairs. The five families above are the ones with exact opposites — the rest combine them, and a handful are genuinely useful.

They are catalogued in Going deeper. You do not need them yet.

Hold each form. Watch the mouse trace under the monitor — that is the work your hand is doing. Then swap the camera and see what the same form does to their screen. Every form is a deal between the two monitors.

Part six

What happens when they reverse?

Back to the gap. The enemy changes direction, and you have not reacted yet — for the next fifth of a second your feet keep doing whatever they were doing.

During that instant, one of two things happens. Your movement can help close the mistake, or it can make the mistake larger.

If your current direction carries you the same way they are now going, the world does part of your correction for you: they stay closer to where your crosshair already points. If it carries you the other way, every one of those milliseconds is adding to the correction you owe.

The figure runs that experiment for all eight keys at once. They reverse, you hold one key for one reaction time, nobody touches the mouse, and the bar is how far off them you end up. The dashed line is what standing still would have cost — anything under it helped, anything over it hurt.

The original guide gives the two cases names: a change of direction that shrinks the coming correction is inward, one that grows it is outward. Every form you met in Part five is one or the other — mirroring and half-sideways mirroring are inward, both anti-mirrors are outward, and back-and-forth is one of each.

Slow your reaction time down and watch the bars stretch. The slower you notice, the more your choice of key matters.

Then why ever go outward?

Inward forms are cheaper for you — and, by symmetry, usually cheaper for them. Outward forms cost you correction but force speed on both players. Which one you want depends on whose skills the fight should test, and that is the next section.

Part seven

Which kind of aim duel do you want?

At this point it is tempting to ask which form is best. That is the wrong question, and the simulation shows why.

Run the same fight with both players equally skilled, and mirroring puts both of you at 100% — the target is frozen for both, nobody misses, nobody wins. Anti-mirroring drops both of you to a quarter. No form made you hit more than them.

What a form actually decides is which skill the fight tests. Mirroring makes the target almost stationary — for both of you — so the fight becomes a plain trade of damage. Anti-mirroring forces both players to track at speed. The diagonal forms demand precision or smoothness in different amounts.

So the real question is: what kind of aim duel do I want this to become?

  • Better tracking than them? Make both players track — anti-mirror.
  • More health, or more damage per second? A plain trade favours you — mirror and let it be a trade.
  • Great precision but weaker at speed? Slow the fight down — half-sideways mirroring.

Give yourself the better mouse control in the figure, and watch which bars move. Mirroring barely reacts — it is the one form that refuses to be about mouse control at all, which is exactly why it suits the player with the resource advantage and betrays the player with the skill advantage.

Part eight

Aiming is not the only job your movement has

Everything so far has treated your feet as servants of your crosshair. They have other work.

Sometimes your movement needs to help your aim. Sometimes it needs to dodge. Sometimes it needs to carry you behind a wall, towards your team, or out of a second enemy's line of fire. Good movement has a purpose — and deciding which job matters most right now is a bigger skill than any form.

Strafe aiming is the default you fall back on, not a contract. You will drop it and pick it back up several times in a single fight, and that is correct play. The reason to practise the forms until they are automatic is precisely so they can keep running underneath while your attention is on something else.

Go somewhere while you dodge

Here is the cheapest improvement available to almost every player. Over a few seconds, drift one way more than the other. Do not finish the fight standing where you started it.

A dodge that oscillates on the spot has a fixed centre, and a fixed centre is a place someone can simply leave their crosshair — you cross it twice per cycle for free. Drift, and that stops working: the centre keeps moving away from wherever they parked. Drifting also makes both of you cover a wider angle, which costs you a little and them more, and it is the only way to actually get somewhere mid-fight — to reach cover, break an angle, or rejoin your team.

Drag their drift to zero in the figure and watch the thick orange appear. That is a crosshair sitting still, being paid for nothing.

The three jobs

No movement does all three at once. Most good movement does two.

Where you stand matters too

Walls, corners and distance change how much room the enemy has and how much of them you can see. That is a whole subject of its own — see the companion guide to geometric positioning.

Checkpoint

You now know enough to use strafe aiming in a real match. Answer their changes of direction. Choose with or against them for a reason. Drift somewhere while you do it. Everything below this line is depth, not prerequisite.

Going deeper

Chains, and the full catalogue

Everything from here is for readers who want the whole framework. It is genuinely elaborate — and none of it is required to benefit from the first eight parts.

The flaw in everything so far

Mirror left and right for four seconds and you end up exactly where you started. So does every form built from two opposite directions — the two halves cancel. You may be aiming beautifully, but you are not going anywhere, and a fight you cannot leave is a fight anyone else may join.

The figure shows four seconds of three forms as tracks on the ground. The first two return to their starting point every cycle, forever. The third — built from a quarter turn instead of a half turn — actually travels.

So: what if you need to keep strafe aiming and move towards cover?

Quarter-turn forms

Answer their left with forward-right and their right with forward-left, and both halves push you towards them: a forward triangle. The backward version retreats. The sideways versions circle. None can be held forever — you eventually reach them, leave, or lap them — which is exactly why they get combined.

Connections: switching forms without breaking the rule

Start in a left-right mirror. At the right moment, replace one side with forward-right. Your local dodge still answers the enemy — but the whole pattern now drifts forward.

That switch was legal because the two forms agreed at the moment you made it: there was one enemy direction for which both forms wanted the same key from you, and you passed through it. The guide calls such a shared prescription a connection, and it is the mechanism behind every combined pattern.

Two facts organise everything. First, no two of the five basic families connect to each other — each one is an island, which is why nothing built purely from them can travel. Second, bring in the quarter-turn forms and the whole set becomes a network you can move through: chains of connected forms that draw triangles, closed rhombi, or circles around the enemy on the ground, while never once breaking the strafe-aiming rule.

Build a chain. The figure only offers forms that legally connect to where you are, with the shared key written on the arrow. Try closing a rhombus, then try circling them.

Why the catalogue is worth reading

Knowing how much mouse a given change of direction demands is a learned skill. Working out why two similar-looking shapes ask for completely different corrections is the training, even before you ever use them in a game.

Advanced forms: playing the player

The forms so far respond to what the enemy does. The advanced ones anticipate what the enemy will do — each is built around a response it deliberately provokes, and holds the answer to it in advance.

That is why they need timing and a feel for how ordinary players react when their aim is under pressure. Calm is a technique, not a temperament

Most players badly overestimate what their opponent can do. In most moments the enemy's options are few and their outcomes span a small range. The players who seem supernaturally calm are mostly players who have made their own picture easier to read: they strafe aim, so the target moves slowly on their screen; they position well, so it has fewer options; they push at chosen moments, so its responses are predictable.

Panic works the other way round. Spam your keys, jump constantly and flick at shadows, and the picture on your own screen becomes fast, erratic and unreadable — and then you play like it. Simplify what you do and you simplify what you see.

The catch is that calm play does not feel like doing anything. Holding a long strafe, or keeping the mouse still at the centre of their dodge, can be the best move available and still feel passive. Confidence in these plays only comes from having seen them work, which is what practice is for.

Watching better players

Playing against stronger players is worth more than copying them. And when you do watch top players, do not analyse details — absorb the overall feel of their fights and try to reproduce that.

Practice

Tying movement to aim takes a long time, and no one should expect it to come quickly. Two principles carry most of the weight.

Practise one thing at a time, deliberately. Pick the thing this session is for — a form, drifting while you dodge, taking an angle — and think about it actively while you play. One-versus-one modes suit this: every round has a pause in which to decide what the next one is testing.

Find somewhere you do not mind looking bad. An alias, an unranked queue, anything. The practice that improves you is the practice where you try things that do not work yet, and you will not try them where the result matters to you.

One habit worth stealing: bind an aim-bot to your fire key in an aim trainer's free play, and move alongside it. The bot has perfect mouse control, so it shows you exactly what the mouse is supposed to do for the form you are learning — the fastest way there is to build a feel for a new one.

Scores mostly measure the wrong thing

A scoring system is a tireless training partner, so if it rewards the wrong movement it teaches the wrong movement efficiently. None of the current systems rewards strafe aiming itself — the comparison below shows what each one actually pays for. Treat scores loosely and the intent exactly.

Notes: the maths, the model, and the sources

Where this comes from

The framework, the terminology, the form catalogue and the routines come from Strafe Aiming 101, published free by AIMER7 in August 2019 — a companion to his earlier guide on geometric positioning. The material on purpose, drifting, control and calm draws on Fundamentals of Strafe by Sam (@400apm), July 2022, itself written to lead readers to the AIMER7 guides. Both are worth reading in full. What this site adds is the simulation: the guides argue in prose, and this page runs the arguments instead.

The assumptions

Everything on the page assumes the eight movement directions are equivalent — same speed, same acceleration — and that you hold a tracking weapon with no spread, recoil or reload. Nobody jumps. Ground speed is 10 units per second because the original guide uses that round number; nothing depends on it. None of these is true of any real game, and all of them can be relaxed once the shape of the idea is clear.

Relative speed across the sight line

Put the line of sight along x. You look along it, the enemy looks back along it, and their left hand points where your right hand points. A held key becomes a world vector by rotating it into that player's frame. Split both velocities into a part across the sight line and a part along it; only the first is what the mouse must follow.

vacross=|s·sinθtheirs−s·sinθyours|

with s the ground speed and θ each key's angle from its own player's forward. Because you face each other, the two contributions enter with opposite signs — which is why pressing the same key doubles the speed and pressing the opposite one cancels it. For the five families this gives 0, 2.93, 10, 17.07 and 20 exactly: mirroring cancels, anti-mirroring adds, a diagonal contributes 10cos45°=7.07, and back-and-forth contributes nothing across at all.

From units per second to your mousepad

ω=vacrossd,centimetres per second=ω·180/π360·cm per 360

Angular rate is relative speed divided by range, so halving the range doubles everything — the reason a form that is comfortable across a room is unplayable at close quarters, and the reason the slow half-sideways mirror is the long-range pick.

Counting the fifty-six

Eight choices for the first half of a form and seven for the second give 8×7=56 ordered pairs. Classified by the angle between your two directions: each direction has one opposite and two neighbours at each of 45°, 90° and 135°, giving 8 + 16 + 16 + 16. One caution: the original guide names two advanced forms "45-forms" after the corner of the triangle they draw on the ground; classified by the turn itself — the guide's own metric everywhere else — they are 135° forms, and this site classifies by the turn throughout.

Inward and outward, as a measurement

The classification is usually given as a rule with cases. This site measures it instead: freeze the crosshair at the instant of reversal, hold a candidate key for one reaction time, and record the angle between crosshair and body; standing still is the baseline. That one experiment reproduces every label in the guide — including back-and-forth coming out mixed, where the deciding factor is the range: pushing in shortens it, and screen speed goes as one over the range.

The aim model

Both simulated players run the same controller. It has two delays, and they are different things: tracking runs on visual data about 70 ms old, while noticing that the target's rate has jumped is cognitive and slow — that is the reaction time the sliders set. Between surprises the hand matches the rate it believes the target has, with a light trim. After a surprise the belief holds its old value for one reaction time while the gap opens at the full relative speed; then one ballistic flick closes it, aimed once, missing by a fixed fraction of its own size, so a bigger correction is also a worse one. Tracking wobble is a settling random walk whose size grows with commanded mouse speed — the only place "mouse control" enters the model.

Nothing in the controller knows what a form is. The forms are inputs, and the accuracy ranking the model produces — mirror, half-sideways mirror, back-and-forth, the anti-mirrors — is the guide's ranking, derived rather than encoded. What the model leaves out: walls and height, jumping, projectile travel, spread, and an enemy who reads you back (the simulated enemy is a dodge script with a period and a drift).

Take this away

Your keyboard changes the amount of aiming your mouse has to do. You cannot react sooner — but before every reversal, your feet have already decided how big the correction will be, which skill the fight is testing, and where you will be standing when it ends. Choose all three on purpose.
